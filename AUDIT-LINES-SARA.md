## Premise check, before any line
The body's last sentence ALREADY says you may be told to change nothing. Ehsan's pick ("If what you have works, you keep it.") says it a second time, as a condition. My own lead this morning ("If nothing needs changing, that goes in writing too") said it a third time. That is why both read as fine and neither reads as better: a paraphrase cannot beat the sentence it paraphrases.
So line one's job is re-read: not "you may change nothing" (said), but "what follows the audit is yours, and none of it requires us." That is the sentence a sales trap can never write. The enemy under every line: the free audit written to need its author.
Line two's job: free, honestly, with a star that now points at real terms. "Limited time" is retail; "for now" carries the same truth in daylight. Budgeted words: "yours" and "keep" go to one line only, never both.

## LINE ONE · ranked · 14 options
1. Yours to act on, with us or without us.
   Why: promise first ("yours"), then the one clause no sales trap can say; a competitor must argue "with us", which is the trap. Lead.
2. What you do next is yours.
   Why: six plain words, unconditional, a child says it back; the gentlest form of the same claim.
3. Nothing in it needs us.
   Why: the bravest line; it says the audit is complete without a purchase. Refusal-led, so it rides second in a promise-led set. Defended hardest.
4. Yours to act on, or to put in a drawer.
   Why: the drawer is the concrete picture of permission; warm, a little funny, never cute.
5. The writing is yours. So is the decision.
   Why: short then shorter, the echo on "yours"; two sentences beat three.
6. Then the next move is yours, with us or without us.
   Why: option 1 with a step word; "then" ties it to the body, costs three words.
7. It ends where you want it to.
   Why: the audit has no hook on the end; soft, promise-led, slightly abstract for a six-year-old.
8. Take it to anyone, including nobody.
   Why: the cleverest; "including nobody" makes the point in two words, but it is wit you might explain once. Demir's call.
9. With us or without us, it still holds.
   Why: the claim is the writing stands alone; "holds" is a shade abstract.
10. You can read it and walk away.
   Why: plain permission; "walk away" leans a touch noir for his hand.
11. Keep it, use it, or forget it.
   Why: three verbs, which is the metronome; kept only because the verbs are a child's.
12. No pitch comes stapled to it.
   Why: true and compact, but it names the industry's habit and sours the daylight.
13. Nobody rings you after unless you ask.
   Why: a real promise, but it describes our behaviour, which the brief says never to explain.
14. Good news is allowed.
   Why: warm, but it is the body sentence again in a new coat; the paraphrase trap.

Cut before ranking: "Read it, keep it, do what you like with it" (three even beats), "You owe us nothing for it" (cost-first), "If it works, it stays" (conditional, Ehsan's shape again).

   Why: the plainest true sentence; a child says it back; no "audit", no retail word.

   Why: keeps the "limited time" truth honestly in two words; the star earns its place.

   Why: as plain as 1, a shade more transactional.

Cut before ranking: "Free this year" (invented fact), "No fee, no catch" (sells), "Free, whatever it finds" and "The audit is free" (this morning's and Ehsan's; the new-angle rule kills unconfirmed lines).
